# Human-likeness
**Type:** Construct  
**Referenced in:** 14 papers  
**Also known as:** Human-like comprehension, Perceived humanlikeness  

## State of the Field

**Definition agreement:** Consensus. Most papers define human-likeness as a model or agent's outputs, behavior, or text resembling human responses or writing, often in terms of naturalness, plausibility, conversational quality, or indistinguishability from humans. A few papers shift the scope to human-like language understanding or human-like learning trajectories, but these are outliers relative to the dominant output- and behavior-focused definition.

**Space of definitions.** Across the papers, the prevailing usage treats human-likeness as resemblance between a model's outputs or behavior and human responses, with emphasis on natural, conversational, believable, or distributionally human patterns rather than only high task accuracy. Papers apply this broad framing to daily activities, role-playing behavior, tutoring responses, and writing style, and one paper makes the contrast explicit by comparing response patterns rather than absolute performance. Smaller lines of work use nearby versions of the construct for language comprehension with human-like depth and nuance, for users' perceived humanlikeness of an agent, or for learning trajectories that mirror human learners, so the main disagreement is whether the construct concerns outputs and behavior, user perception, comprehension, or developmental dynamics.

**Space of facets.** Papers break the construct into different facets depending on setting: naturalness, coherence, and plausibility for generated daily activities; natural, conversational tutoring responses that do not sound robotic; realism and believability in role-playing, especially emotional expressiveness and personality consistency; and lexical diversity, flexible wording, and stylistic naturalness in writing. One paper contrasts it with absolute task performance by defining it through similarity to human response distributions, while another extends it to learning trajectories evaluated through distributional signatures. The naming is not fully stable, because some authors use related labels such as human-like comprehension or perceived humanlikeness for narrower variants.

**Space of measurement.** It is linked to 0 measurement instruments and 0 behavioral indicators. Even without named instruments, papers operationalize the construct by comparing model and human response patterns rather than absolute performance, rating daily activities for naturalness, coherence, and plausibility, judging whether a tutor response sounds natural rather than robotic, asking users how humanlike the agent was, and examining human-like word distributions, flexible choices of words and phrases, or learning trajectories. Across papers, these operationalizations follow the local definition in each study, but they target different objects: response patterns, generated behavior, perceived humanlikeness of the agent, text style, or developmental patterns.

**Links.** The only explicit link is to 1 construct, Emotional expression. In a role-playing setting, one paper treats Human-likeness as related to Emotional expression: human-likeness concerns realism, believability, and dynamic, emotionally engaging interactions, while Emotional expression concerns vivid expression of emotion.
