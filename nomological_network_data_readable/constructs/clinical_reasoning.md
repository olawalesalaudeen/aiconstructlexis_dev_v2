# Clinical reasoning
**Type:** Construct  
**Referenced in:** 6 papers  

## State of the Field

**Definition agreement:** Complementary. Most papers define clinical reasoning as an ability or cognitive process of applying medical knowledge to patient information in order to reach diagnoses, management choices, or other clinical decisions, with differences in emphasis such as context integration, calculations, or updating on new evidence. One paper frames it more narrowly as supporting inference over medical codes and records for prediction and decision-making, which is a data-centered facet of the same overall phenomenon rather than a contradictory one.

**Space of definitions.** Across the papers, the prevailing usage treats clinical reasoning as a latent ability or cognitive process for applying medical knowledge and logical inference to patient information in order to reach diagnoses, management decisions, or other clinical judgments, rather than simple factual recall. Definitions commonly emphasize integrating context, pathophysiology, clinical data, and new evidence, and one paper frames it as interactive reasoning in which a system decides whether it has enough information to answer confidently (MediQ: Question-Asking LLMs and a Benchmark for Reliable Interactive Clinical Reasoning). A smaller line of work emphasizes particular settings for the same construct, especially structured medical calculations within clinical workflows and inference over medical codes and patient records for prediction and decision-making.

**Space of facets.** Papers break the construct into several facets: analyzing patient information, identifying knowledge gaps, integrating new evidence, and supporting diagnosis or decision-making, with some definitions also adding management decisions and clinical judgment. Another recurring subtype is workflow-specific reasoning through calculation steps, described as selecting the correct formula, extracting clinical variables, and performing numerical computations. Some work also uses the term for inference over medical codes and patient records, while the MCQ-focused discussion contrasts clinical reasoning with factual medical knowledge and with benchmark vulnerability to pattern variations or test-taking strategies.

**Space of measurement.** No paper links clinical reasoning to a measurement instrument or behavioral indicator. Even so, papers invoke it in several task settings: MCQ-based medical evaluations, interactive question-asking tasks where the system must decide at each turn whether it has enough information, evidence-based medical calculation tasks requiring formula selection, variable extraction, and numerical computation, and work on inference over medical codes and patient records. Papers do not explicitly link these settings to named measurement instruments or behavioral indicators.
