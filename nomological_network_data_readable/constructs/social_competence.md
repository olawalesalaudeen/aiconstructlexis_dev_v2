# Social competence
**Type:** Construct  
**Referenced in:** 3 papers  
**Also known as:** Social awareness, Social understanding  

## State of the Field

**Definition agreement:** Complementary. The papers define social competence as different aspects of an AI system's social ability in language use: one emphasizes producing socially appropriate replies, another handling socially grounded judgments or situations, and another interpreting implicit social cues and intentions. These are not the exact same scope, but they describe compatible parts of the same broader phenomenon of socially competent language behavior and understanding.

**Space of definitions.** Across the definitions, the shared framing is a latent social ability in language or dialogue, but papers do not converge on a single emphasis. One usage centers on whether a conversational agent responds appropriately in social interaction and avoids socially deficient replies; another treats the construct as the ability to handle socially grounded judgments or commonsense social situations in language tasks; a third focuses on recognizing subtle, implicit social cues, intentions, and implications beyond the literal text. The main variation is whether the construct is framed as appropriateness of produced responses, performance on socially grounded judgments, or interpretation of implicit social meaning.

**Space of facets.** Papers do not present a common subtype scheme. Instead, they use several neighboring labels, social competence, social awareness, and social understanding, with each label foregrounding a different aspect: appropriate response behavior in interaction, handling socially grounded or commonsense social situations, and interpreting implicit cues such as intentions and implications. One paper also places deficiencies in social competence within a broader category of conversational errors, alongside logical inconsistencies.

**Space of measurement.** Operationalization is narrow in the available work: the construct is linked to 1 measurement instrument, SIQA, and to no behavioral indicators. One paper uses SIQA within a set of tasks described as examining models' "social awareness capabilities," so the explicit measurement tracks task performance on socially grounded judgments. This operationalization aligns most directly with the social awareness framing, and the available evidence does not show separate instruments for the conversational-response or implicit-cue framings.
