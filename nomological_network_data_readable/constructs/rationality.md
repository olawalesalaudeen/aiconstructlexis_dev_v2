# Rationality
**Type:** Construct  
**Referenced in:** 10 papers  

## State of the Field

**Definition agreement:** Complementary. Most papers define rationality as a property of an agent or model's decision-making, but they foreground different aspects of that same phenomenon: some emphasize choosing goal- or payoff-optimal actions, while others emphasize being guided by reason through logical consistency, probabilistic coherence, factual grounding, or an internal sense of utility. Because these definitions apply to the same kind of thing and can be combined without contradiction, they are better seen as complementary than as a single consensus definition or a conflict.

**Space of definitions.** Across these papers, the prevailing usage treats rationality as a property of model or agent decision-making, centered on choosing actions that are payoff-maximizing, goal-optimal, or aligned with logical self-interest. A smaller line of work defines it by conformity to normative reasoning standards, describing decisions and inferences as guided by evidence, logic, and probabilistic principles under uncertainty; one paper further ties it to an internally learned sense of utility from posterior experience rather than external performance measures, and another explicitly contrasts it with human-like emotional deviation.

**Space of facets.** When papers break the construct down, the clearest facet structure presents rationality through Information Grounding, Logical Consistency, Invariance from Irrelevant Context, and Orderability of Preference. Across definitions, papers also use the term at different levels: as a latent quality or ability, as a tendency to choose optimal strategies, and as an extent of adherence to normative standards; one paper contrasts it with intelligence, framing intelligence as raw cognitive ability and rationality as adherence to normative reasoning standards.

**Space of measurement.** No measurement instrument and no behavioral indicator are linked to rationality. In practice, papers describe it through task behavior such as more rational decision-making across emotions, providing optimal solutions from internal utility judgment, or "more rational behavior" defined as achieving a Nash equilibrium by contributing fewer tokens, and one paper also refers to cognitive psychology tasks used to assess rationality; these descriptions reflect both goal-optimal choice and adherence to normative reasoning.

**Links.** Rationality is linked to one construct, Commonsense reasoning. One paper claims a causal relationship from Commonsense reasoning to rationality, stating that the system "engages in human common-sense reasoning for rationality in this stage"; papers do not report correlational or non-causal related links here.
