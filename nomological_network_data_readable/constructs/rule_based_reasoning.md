# Rule-based reasoning
**Type:** Construct  
**Referenced in:** 6 papers  
**Also known as:** Rule application  

## State of the Field

**Definition agreement:** Too little evidence.

**Space of definitions.** Across the papers, the term is used in two distinct ways rather than one prevailing one. One framing treats it as a latent ability to understand, integrate, and apply multiple abstract rules simultaneously, with examples such as counting, color logic, shape constraints, path-finding, and step-by-step planning with arithmetic and geometric rules in a constrained spatial task. Another framing uses the nearby label rule application for applying sport-specific rules to a concrete scenario or video, described as connecting movements with context-based rules to determine the correct judgment.

**Space of facets.** Where papers use the broader abstract framing, they present the construct as combining several rule types in one task. A narrower usage centers on domain-specific rule application in sports. One paper also places inductive reasoning within a rule-based reasoning paradigm: the rule is first derived from examples and then applied to new questions.

**Space of measurement.** Operationalization is sparse. Papers link the construct to one measurement instrument, SPaRC, and no behavioral indicators. The linked instrument measures it through a spatial pathfinding challenge that combines path-finding, counting, and logic over colors and shapes under complex conditions, including step-by-step planning with arithmetic and geometric rules. This operationalization matches the abstract multi-rule framing, and no measurement instrument is linked to the sports-specific rule-application usage.

**Links.** Beyond measurement, papers connect it to three constructs. One paper relates inductive reasoning to rule-based reasoning, describing induction as deriving the correct rule from examples and then using that rule on new questions within a rule-based reasoning paradigm. The same paper claims a causal relationship from rule-based reasoning to knowledge transfer, stating that once a rule is formed through induction it should be transferable. Another paper relates mathematical reasoning to rule-based reasoning through tasks that involve precise rule following.
