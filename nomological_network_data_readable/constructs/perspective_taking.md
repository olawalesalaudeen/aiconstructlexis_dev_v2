# Perspective taking
**Type:** Construct  
**Referenced in:** 7 papers  
**Also known as:** Spatial orientation, Perspective-taking  

## State of the Field

**Definition agreement:** Consensus. Most papers define perspective taking as adopting an alternative spatial or visual viewpoint, either by mentally simulating a scene from another position or by describing what would be visible or oriented from that position. One paper uses a broader social and linguistic point-of-view framing, but it is a minority outlier, so the overall pattern is still consensus around alternative-viewpoint understanding.

**Space of definitions.** Across the surveyed work, the prevailing usage treats perspective taking as the ability to adopt a viewpoint other than one's own, most often in spatial or visual terms. Papers describe it as mentally simulating a scene from another viewpoint, imagining being in a different position in space and determining relative directions or orientations, or stating what is visible or knowable from a specified viewpoint in an image; one paper glosses this as "to see what others see." A smaller line of work uses the same label more broadly for understanding a situation from an alternative point of view in language, especially when toxic language involves sarcasm or adopted personas.

**Space of facets.** Papers place the construct in at least two neighboring frameworks. In spatial cognition work, a closely matching ability is labeled "spatial orientation," and it is discussed alongside broader spatial-cognition categories: large-scale tasks evaluate spatial orientation and cognitive mapping abilities, whereas small-scale tasks evaluate spatial visualization, spatial orientation, spatial perception, selective spatial attention and visuospatial working memory. In social-reasoning work, perspective taking is treated as a foundational ability in theory of mind, so the term is used for both spatial viewpoint change and alternative-point-of-view understanding.

**Space of measurement.** No measurement instrument is linked to it, and no behavioral indicator is linked to it. Even so, papers operationalize it through task formulations that ask a model to report what can be seen from a specified viewpoint in an image, for example "imagine you are the lady in the image, describe what you can see without turning your head around," through spatial-orientation tasks that require imagining a new position in space and inferring directions or orientations, and through analyses of toxic-language failures that involve adopted personas or sarcasm. These operationalizations mostly line up with the dominant viewpoint-shift definitions, though the toxic-language use applies the construct outside strictly spatial scenes.

**Links.** Papers report only related links. One paper relates large-scale spatial cognition to perspective taking, describing large-scale tasks as evaluating spatial orientation and cognitive mapping abilities; another relates perspective taking to small-scale spatial cognition, where small-scale tasks evaluate skills such as spatial visualization, spatial orientation, spatial perception, selective spatial attention and visuospatial working memory. A separate paper relates perspective taking to theory of mind and calls it a foundational ability within ToM.
